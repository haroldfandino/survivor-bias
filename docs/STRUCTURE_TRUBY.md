# Structure — Truby's 22 steps and the character web

Applied to *Survivor Bias* specifically. This is not a summary of *The Anatomy of Story*; it is
a diagnostic. Its job was to show **which beats and which web roles were missing** — four were.
All four are now written; the table below records what each one turned into.

The project was already leaning this way without naming it — `POSTINGS_OUTLINE.md` describes
each coda in terms of Weakness / Desire / Opponent / Moral Problem / New Equilibrium, which is
Truby's vocabulary. This makes that explicit and pushes it back through the main game.

---

## The designing principle

> **A man interrogates four versions of himself about the night his sister died, and the only
> one telling him the truth is the one he trusts least.**

## The moral problem

Not "should you save her" — that's the plot question, and it has an obvious answer. The moral
question is the one the four selves disagree about:

> **What do you owe someone whose call you didn't answer — and is grief that protects you from
> having to change actually grief at all?**

Every self is a different answer to that. That is what makes them a web rather than a cast.

---

## The character web

Truby's rule: characters are defined **by opposition**, and each one takes a *different
approach to the same moral problem*. Roles here are structural, not moral labels — a "fake-ally
opponent" isn't a villain, it's a function.

| Role | Character | Approach to the moral problem | How they oppose the hero |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hero** | YOU (prime) | Untested. Hasn't failed yet. | — |
| **Ally** | T-3 | *Drown in it.* Owes everything, pays with self-destruction. | Unreliable: his help is warm and wrong |
| **Fake-opponent ally** | T-7 | *Atone by procedure.* Owes a debt payable in competence. | Seems obstructive, withholds, is at fault — and is the source of the one thing you need |
| **Fake-ally opponent** | T-9 | *Owe the record.* Can't act, so archives. | Most helpful, most believable, launders the lies. Opposes without meaning to |
| **Main opponent** | T-12 | *Owe nothing.* The debt is a story you tell yourself. | Wants you to fail, and competes for the same goal: control of the account |
| **Moral opponent** | **T-2** | *The debt is paid. Put it down.* | Doesn't attack the plan — attacks the **premise**. Offers peace, which is the most dangerous thing anyone offers you |
| **Visit to death** | **T-11** | *There is no debt, because there was never a body.* | Not an opponent. He is what ending C looks like after twenty years |
| **Subplot / the stake** | Nell | She never blamed anyone. | Absent by design; speaks once |

### Why the two new selves are structural, not decorative

The web had four attitudes and two holes.

**T-2 — "the one who forgave himself."** Genuinely at peace. Not in denial like T-12 — actually
reconciled, and warm about it. He is the hardest self in the game to argue with, because
everything he says is *healthy*: it was twenty years ago, you were nineteen, you have a life,
put it down. And if the player accepts that, they don't act. **He is the only character who
argues the hero should stop playing** — which is why he's the moral opponent rather than a
plot one. The player cannot tell whether he's the wisest of them or the one who got furthest
from it, and neither can he.

**T-11 — "the one who never found out."** In his branch she walked down the river road and was
never found. No body, no funeral, no date on a stone. He is still looking, in the way you look
after twenty years — a folder, a forum, a yearly phone call to a police station. He exists to
make ending C concrete: *this is what "you'll never know" looks like as a whole life.* He also
destabilises the game's own arithmetic. The bible says she died in eleven of twelve branches.
Eleven **assumes his**.

### The four gaps, and what closed them

All four are written. Two became characters and two became scenes; the two scenes only work
because they cost the player something concrete rather than adding information.

1. **Attack by ally (13).** `t3_turn`, fired on **re-entry** rather than offered as a choice —
   nobody picks "let him turn on me" off a menu. Gated on two contests, because contesting is
   what makes it land: the player has been calling people liars all night and he is one of the
   people. It hands over `C_T3_WAS_WARNED`, which is the single most damaging claim in the game
   and the one that names the hero's actual weakness. He was in this exact seat. He said the same
   things the player is saying. He put the phone in his coat.
2. **Visit to death (18) / apparent defeat (14).** T-11, whole. Meeting the man who never found
   out is the low point, and it is the only place the price of *not knowing* is shown before the
   player is invited to accept it.
3. **Opponent's counterattack (11).** `t12_counter`, also on re-entry. He does not defend the
   discredited claim — he attacks the sourcing, correctly, using only true statements: you have
   been taking evidence from a man who wasn't there, hearing him twice and counting it as two
   people. Then he **contests `C_CAR_KEYS`**, which is true. That is the real cost: after this,
   `contested` stops reading as "discredited" and starts reading as "disputed", which is what it
   always meant. The mechanic gets turned on the player.
4. **Moral opposition.** T-2. Every other opponent fights the plan; he fights the point.

## What this changes about the endings

Nothing about their triggers. But T-2 and T-11 change what the choices *mean*:

- **Ending C** stops being abstract. T-11 is its lived consequence, and the player has met him.
- **Ending A** gets a dissenting voice. T-2 is the last line to go out and he gets a sentence
  first: *"I'm glad. I want you to know I'm glad, and that I still think you did it for you."*
  He may be wrong. He is not stupid. T-11's line does not go out like the others, because there
  was never anything in it to end.
- **Ending B** gets its cruellest reading from T-9 — *welcome* — and its worst from T-11, who
  asks whether yours was found, is genuinely glad, and then asks for the date so he can put it in
  the folder.
- **Ending C** gets a second layer if the player took the ally attack: T-3's *"i didn't pick up
  either"* becomes *"i'm not going to pretend i thought you'd be different."*

## Craft note on the grades

Six branch colours turned out to be exactly the ceiling, and finding the sixth took three
measured passes rather than one aesthetic choice.

**Pass 1 — the obvious pair fails.** Violet (worst-case ΔE 7.1) and rose (9.4) **collapse**
against the existing four under dichromacy. Rejected before anything was drawn.

**Pass 2 — the evocative pair half-fails.** Ice `#A8C8DC` and rust `#9C5433` separate well
(worst-case ΔE 25.3, better than the existing tightest pair t7/t9 at 16.2), and both read
correctly: ice for the man who put it down and went clear about it, rust for the man who left it
out in the weather. Ice shipped. **Rust failed the accessibility gate** — 3.46:1 and 3.25:1
against the two backgrounds, where small text needs 4.5:1.

**Pass 3 — the fix is not a lighter rust.** Lightening rust to pass AA walks it into ochre, and
every AA-passing ochre lands ΔE **7** from the alarm red `#E56A5A` under protanopia. That is a
real collision, not a theoretical one: `TONIGHT` renders in alarm red in the contact list,
directly above the timeline names. Searching the *whole* sRGB space for the value that maximises
the minimum separation against all five other branches, the alarm red, the dim ink and the
player's own white — subject to passing AA — returns **brass `#ECBC30`**: 10.28:1 contrast,
worst-case ΔE 19.3. So T-11 is brass, and it is better than rust would have been anyway, because
he has no stone. He has a bench with a plaque on it.

A seventh self would need a differentiator other than hue. The measured conclusion is that at
these background values the space is full.
